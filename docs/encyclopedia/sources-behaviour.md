# Source dossier: wing behaviour (collapse, stall, active flying, EN ratings, aspect ratio)

Compiled 8 Oct 2026 for five paraglidingatlas.com encyclopedia answer pages. Every fact below comes from a page that was actually fetched. Each fact gives: the fact in my words, a short verbatim excerpt, the source title, publisher, URL, and licence status.

## How to use this file

- **Paraphrase, do not copy.** The excerpts are here so the writer can check they have kept the meaning. Only the FAA and NASA material is public domain. Everything else is copyrighted, so use the facts only.
- **Spot-check before publishing.** The excerpts came through a web-fetch tool that converts pages and PDFs to text. PDF text extraction can change hyphens, spacing and quote marks. Where the tool had to split a long sentence, the pieces were consecutive and are joined here with a space. Check the original wording of any quote you plan to print word for word.
- **Licence labels:**
  - **PD-USGov** = public domain US government work (17 U.S.C. 105).
  - **(c) facts only** = copyrighted. Paraphrase the facts and cite the source.
  - **CC BY-SA** = Wikipedia text licence.
- **Search budget.** The shared web-search budget ran out partway through. Sources that never appeared in a search result could not be fetched: the BHPA, USHPA and FFVL pages, the Nova, Phi and Skywalk manuals, the main FAA PHAK chapter 5 PDF, CEN's own catalogue page, and flyozone.com product pages. These gaps are listed at the end of each section.
- **Related questions in question-bank.csv.** These facts also cover: what happens if a paraglider collapses, asymmetric vs frontal collapse, what a parachutal stall is, what a spin is, low B vs high B, and moving up from EN A to EN B.

## Q1. Why does a paraglider collapse?

### Mechanism

1. **The wing has no rigid structure, so turbulence can fold part of it.**
   - Excerpt: "Due to the flexible form of a paraglider, turbulence may cause a portion of the wing suddenly to collapse."
   - Source: S6, Delta 4 manual EN v1.0, Ozone, https://cdn1.flyozone.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/1/2018/07/Delta-4-manual-EN-v1.0.pdf
   - Licence: (c) facts only

2. **A collapse follows a drop in angle of attack. A frontal collapse happens when the angle of attack goes strongly negative.**
   - Excerpt: "A frontal collapse occurs when the leading edge collapses symmetrically due to a high negative angle of attack."
   - Source: S12, Bolero 8 User Manual v1.0, Gin Gliders, https://static.gingliders.com/paragliders/bolero-8/documents/bolero-8-user-manual-en.pdf
   - Licence: (c) facts only

3. **Sinking air (a downdraught) pushes the leading edge down and unloads the wing. The pilot feels this first as a loss of pressure in the brakes.**
   - Excerpt: "As a down draught hits the leading edge, pushing it downwards and unloading the wing, you should sense the drop off in pressure through the brakes."
   - Source: S36, Paragliding techniques: How to handle a collapse, Cross Country Magazine, https://xcmag.com/news/blowout/
   - Licence: (c) facts only

4. **Braking briefly raises the angle of attack and stops it going negative, which prevents the collapse. The pilot must then release the brake so the wing does not stall.** This shows both failure modes on one axis: too low an angle of attack collapses the wing, and too high stalls it.
   - Excerpt: "In doing so you radically increase the angle of attack in the wing for an instant, stop the angle going negative, which prevent the paraglider from collapsing, then immediately lift your hands back up to avoid stalling."
   - Source: S36, as above
   - Licence: (c) facts only

5. **The canopy keeps its shape because air is trapped inside it.** This is why most front tucks reopen quickly.
   - Excerpt: "the wing has air trapped inside it, and keeps its shape."
   - Source: S40, Paraglider Control: Stall, Spin, Collapse!, Flybubble, https://flybubble.com/blog/paraglider-control-stall-spin-collapse
   - Licence: (c) facts only

### Asymmetric vs frontal collapse

6. **Frontal collapse definition.**
   - Excerpt: "A frontal collapse is when the leading edge of the wing deflates."
   - Source: S46, Safety Investigation Report 20/153, Civil Aviation Authority of New Zealand, https://www.aviation.govt.nz/assets/publications/fatal-accident-reports/20-153-fatal-paraglider-accident-mount-cheeseman-canterbury.pdf
   - Licence: NZ Crown copyright, facts only

7. **Asymmetric collapse definition: one side deflates, which adds drag and turns the wing towards that side. The pilot's body tilts with it, which can tighten the turn.**
   - Excerpt: "An asymmetrical collapse is when one side of the wing deflates, creating drag and inducing a turn in that direction."
   - Second excerpt: "This causes the pilot to tilt, which can exacerbate the turn towards the collapsed side of the wing."
   - Source: S46, as above
   - Licence: NZ Crown copyright, facts only

8. **The main danger after an asymmetric collapse is the spiral dive that can follow, more than the collapse itself.**
   - Excerpt: "It is not so much the collapse that is the problem but the spiral dive afterwards."
   - Source: S35, Active Flying (Bruce Goldsmith, former paragliding world champion), Cross Country Magazine, https://xcmag.com/other/active-flying/
   - Licence: (c) facts only

### Cravats

9. **A cravat is a wingtip caught in the lines, usually after a collapse.**
   - Excerpt: "If the tip of your wing gets stuck in the lines, this is called a 'cravat'."
   - Source: S6, Delta 4 manual, Ozone (URL above)
   - Supporting source: S13, HOOK 6 manual, Niviuk: "A cravat may happen after an asymmetric collapse, when the end of the wing is trapped between the lines." https://niviuk.com/biblioteca/items/449_A/user_manual_hook_6_glider_niviuk_paragliders.pdf
   - Licence: (c) facts only

10. **Big collapses often come with a cravat, and the cravat adds drag.**
    - Excerpt: "Big collapses are often accompanied by a cravat which creates drag"
    - Source: S45, Dealing with big collapses, SkyNomad Paragliding School, https://www.skynomad.com/dealing-with-big-collapses
    - Licence: (c) facts only

11. **Cravat recovery: first stabilise the glider and stop the turn. Then clear the tip with the stabilo line or with deep pumps on the cravated side while leaning away. A full stall is the last resort.**
    - Excerpt (Ozone): "use strong deep pumps of the brake on the cravated side." / "When doing so it is important to lean away from the cravat otherwise you risk spinning or deepening the spiral." / "If it is a very large cravat and the above options have not worked then a full stall is the next option."
    - Source: S6, Delta 4 manual, Ozone
    - Excerpt (Advance): "Stop an increase in rotation rate by just the necessary amount of outside brake." / "Then open the cravated wingtip by pulling the orange stabilo line."
    - Source: S10, ALPHA 8 manual, Flight characteristics, Advance, https://manual.advance.ch/en/alpha_8/4009
    - Licence: (c) facts only

### What makes collapses more likely

12. **Turbulence, especially when the pilot is not flying actively.**
    - Excerpt: "strong turbulent air may cause the wing to collapse asymmetrically in very strong turbulence, especially if you do not fly actively and prevent the collapse."
    - Source: S13, HOOK 6 manual, Niviuk
    - Licence: (c) facts only

13. **Frontal collapses are typical in strong turbulence and when entering or leaving strong thermals.**
    - Excerpt: "A frontal collapse may occur in strong turbulent conditions, entering or exiting powerful thermals."
    - Source: S13, HOOK 6 manual, Niviuk
    - Licence: (c) facts only

14. **Speed bar: accelerating lowers the angle of attack, so the wing collapses more easily. Avoid it near the ground and in turbulence.**
    - Excerpt: "Using the accelerator decreases the angle of attack and makes the wing more prone to collapse, therefore using the accelerator near the ground or in turbulent conditions should be avoided."
    - Source: S6, Delta 4 manual, Ozone
    - Supporting source: S45, SkyNomad: "The use of the speed bar reduces the angle of attack and makes the wing easier to collapse when airflow hits it from above."
    - Licence: (c) facts only

15. **Braking while on the speed bar makes a collapse more likely.** This is how the brief's "small brake inputs on some modern wings" point is supported. The fetched sources only support it for accelerated flight.
    - Excerpt: "Never apply the brakes whilst using the speed system - it makes the wing more prone to collapse."
    - Source: S6, Delta 4 manual, Ozone (the same line appears in S7, LM7 manual)
    - Excerpt (LM7): "using the brakes whilst accelerated weakens the profile and can actually lead to a collapse."
    - Source: S7, LM7 manual EN v1.0, Ozone, https://cdn1.flyozone.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/1/2019/12/LM7-manual-EN-v1.0.pdf
    - Licence: (c) facts only

16. **Lee-side turbulence and rotor behind terrain caused the collapse in a fatal New Zealand accident.**
    - Excerpt: "This lee side turbulence is the most probable cause of the initial frontal collapse"
    - Second excerpt: "Formation of lee side rotor just above the height of the thermal."
    - Source: S46, CAA NZ Safety Investigation Report 20/153
    - Licence: NZ Crown copyright, facts only

17. **Wake turbulence from helicopters has also caused paraglider collapses.** Optional side fact.
    - Excerpt: "wake turbulence from helicopters led to collapses and thus to paraglider crashes."
    - Source: S49, A danger not only for paragliders, Aero-Club der Schweiz, https://aeroclub.ch/en/eine-gefahr-nicht-nur-fur-gleitschirmflieger
    - Licence: (c) facts only

18. **Real collapses are often harder to handle than the induced collapses in certification tests, in every class.**
    - Excerpt: "In the air, paragliders of all classes often demonstrate more demanding reactions to „real" collapses than when collapses are induced in a test program."
    - Source: S18, Safety Journal ("Competition performance with certified safety?", Karl Slezak), DHV, https://dhv.de/en/safety/safety-journal
    - Licence: (c) facts only

19. **No wing is immune to collapse.**
    - Excerpt: "No pilot and no glider are immune to collapses however correct active flying will reduce the chances significantly."
    - Source: S6, Delta 4 manual, Ozone
    - Licence: (c) facts only

20. **Wings built to resist collapse can recover more violently when they do collapse.**
    - Excerpt: "But when collapse-resistant wings finally collapse, the recovery can be more violent than softer wings."
    - Source: S41, Paragliders: Safety, Flybubble, https://flybubble.com/blog/paragliders-safety
    - Licence: (c) facts only

### What the pilot does (manufacturer advice)

21. **Asymmetric collapse, Ozone: shift weight away from the collapse and use only enough brake on the open side to hold your direction. That alone usually brings the wing back.**
    - Excerpt: "Asymmetric collapses should be controlled by weight shifting away from the collapse and applying enough brake to control your direction."
    - Supporting excerpt (LM7): "This action alone will be enough for a full recovery of the wing most of the time."
    - Source: S6 (Delta 4) and S7 (LM7), Ozone
    - Licence: (c) facts only

22. **Asymmetric collapse, Niviuk: shift weight to the open side, brake lightly on that side if needed, and let the collapsed side reopen. If it does not, pull the collapsed-side brake fully and release it at once, repeating if needed. Do not over-brake the flying side.**
    - Excerpt: "Weight-shift toward the open, flying side (the opposite side of the collapse) to keep the wing flying straight, while applying light brake pressure to that side if necessary."
    - Second excerpt: "Do not over-brake or slow down the flying side of the wing (control the turn)."
    - Source: S13, HOOK 6 manual, Niviuk
    - Licence: (c) facts only

23. **Asymmetric collapse, Gin: steer with weight shift and light brake on the open side. Let the wing reopen on its own, pump smoothly only if it does not, and release the speed bar first if you were accelerated.**
    - Excerpt: "Control direction with weight-shift and light brake on the open side." / "If the collapsed side does not reopen, apply smooth, progressive pumping on the collapsed side." / "If the collapse occurs while accelerated, immediately release the speed bar, then apply the standard recovery technique."
    - Source: S12, Bolero 8 User Manual, Gin Gliders
    - Licence: (c) facts only

24. **Asymmetric collapse, Advance: the wing normally reopens by itself. Be careful with the open-side brake and never force the reopening with heavy brake, because that risks a full stall.**
    - Excerpt: "Normally, the wing will reopen without pilot action." / "do not forcibly encourage reopening by the use of excessive brake, because of the risk of a full stall."
    - Source: S10, ALPHA 8 manual, Advance
    - Licence: (c) facts only

25. **Overreacting can stall or spin the wing because it loses airspeed.**
    - Excerpt: "an overreaction can lead to stall or spin due to airspeed loss"
    - Source: S45, SkyNomad
    - Licence: (c) facts only

26. **Frontal collapse: most wings reopen without help.** The manufacturers disagree on whether brake helps. See the disagreements below.
    - Ozone excerpt: "Symmetrical collapses reinflate without pilot input, however 15 to 20cm of brake applied symmetrically will speed the process." (S6)
    - Advance excerpt: "Wait, without applying brake, until the wing is again above you and returns to normal flight." (S10)
    - Niviuk excerpt: "Frontal collapses usually re-inflate without the glider turning." (S13)
    - Gin excerpt: "For recovery, allow the glider to regain speed." / "Avoid deep or prolonged braking." (S12)
    - Licence: (c) facts only

### Disagreements and cautions (Q1)

- **Frontal collapse recovery:**
  - Ozone says 15 to 20 cm of symmetric brake speeds up reinflation.
  - Niviuk says a quick deep pump of both brakes speeds it up: "a symmetrically applied quick braking action with a quick deep pump of both brakes will accelerate the re-inflation" (S13).
  - Advance says wait without applying brake.
  - Gin says avoid deep or prolonged braking.
  - On the page, say the wing usually reopens by itself and that the manual for your own wing decides the rest.
- **How to pump an asymmetric collapse open:**
  - Niviuk: one full (100%) pull on the collapsed side, released at once.
  - Advance: "a deep, fast but brief pull".
  - Gin: "smooth, progressive pumping".
  - UP: "a few deep pumps" and warns "Small hectic brake input has no effect and may even worsen the situation." (S15)
  - All four agree that you pump only if the wing does not reopen by itself.
- **Terminology conflict in a government source:** the CAA NZ paramotor brief (S48) calls an asymmetric collapse "a type of aerodynamic stall". The manuals describe a collapse as a low or negative angle of attack, which is the opposite end from a stall. Do not use the CAA NZ framing.

### Could not verify (Q1)

- **A "gust from behind" or tailwind gust as a collapse trigger.** No fetched source says this. The sources do support sinking air or a downdraught hitting the leading edge (S36), air hitting the wing from above while on speed bar (S45), and a negative angle of attack (S12).
- **No BHPA, USHPA or FFVL text on collapse mechanism or recovery was fetched.** The USHPA document fetched (S50) is only a list of fatalities, for example "suffered a thermic induced collapse, which he was unable to recover from". The federation and safety sources used here are DHV (S18), CAA NZ (S46) and AeCS (S49).

## Q2. What is a stall on a paraglider?

### Aerodynamics (FAA, public domain)

1. **A wing stalls at its critical angle of attack, whatever the airspeed, attitude or weight.**
   - Excerpt: "The angle of attack at which a wing stalls regardless of airspeed, flight attitude, or weight."
   - Source: S1, Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge, Glossary ("Critical angle of attack"), FAA, https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/21_phak_glossary.pdf
   - Licence: PD-USGov

2. **Angle of attack definition.**
   - Excerpt: "It is the angle that is formed by the chord of the airfoil and the direction of the relative wind or between the chord line and the flight path."
   - Source: S1, PHAK Glossary, FAA
   - Licence: PD-USGov

3. **Going past the critical angle of attack always stalls the wing.**
   - Excerpt: "Exceeding the critical AOA for a particular airfoil section will always result in a stall of that section."
   - Source: S2, AC 61-67C Stall and Spin Awareness Training, FAA, https://www.faa.gov/documentlibrary/media/advisory_circular/ac_61-67c.pdf
   - Licence: PD-USGov

4. **A stall can happen at any airspeed and in any attitude.**
   - Excerpt: "A STALL CAN OCCUR AT ANY AIRSPEED, IN ANY ATTITUDE, AT ANY POWER SETTING."
   - Source: S2, AC 61-67C, FAA
   - Supporting source: S3, Glider Flying Handbook ch. 3, FAA: "A stall occurs whenever the angle between the chord line and relative wind exceeds the critical AOA." and "Stalls may occur at any airspeed and in any flight attitude." https://www.faa.gov/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/glider_handbook/gfh_chapter_3.pdf
   - Licence: PD-USGov

5. **Recovery always starts by lowering the angle of attack.**
   - Excerpt: "The key factor in recovering from a stall is regaining positive control of the aircraft by reducing the AOA."
   - Source: S2, AC 61-67C, FAA
   - Licence: PD-USGov

6. **In a spin, one wing is more stalled than the other.**
   - Excerpt: "A spin may develop as a complex, uncoordinated flight maneuver in which one wing becomes more stalled than the other."
   - Source: S3, Glider Flying Handbook ch. 3, FAA
   - Licence: PD-USGov

### How a paraglider is stalled

7. **A full stall comes from pulling both brakes down slowly and evenly. On a paraglider the brakes are what raise the angle of attack.**
   - Excerpt: "Entry into a full stall is achieved by progressively and symmetrically pulling down both brake lines."
   - Source: S10, ALPHA 8 manual, Advance, https://manual.advance.ch/en/alpha_8/4009
   - Licence: (c) facts only

8. **Too much brake causes a full stall. Holding deep brake in rough air can stall the wing by accident.**
   - Excerpt: "Too much brake and you'll enter a full stall."
   - Source: S40, Flybubble, https://flybubble.com/blog/paraglider-control-stall-spin-collapse
   - Excerpt (Ozone): "Avoid flying with continuous amounts of deep brake in rough air as you could inadvertently stall the wing"
   - Source: S6, Delta 4 manual, Ozone
   - Licence: (c) facts only

9. **Turning at minimum speed, with full brakes on, risks a spin.**
   - Excerpt: "Never initiate a turn at minimum speed (i.e. with full brakes on) as you could risk entering a spin."
   - Source: S6, Delta 4 manual, Ozone
   - Licence: (c) facts only

### Full stall

10. **A full stall is when the wing loses its airflow completely and deforms. To recover, keep the brake input even and controlled, and release fully once the canopy has reinflated and is in front of you. If it has dropped behind, hold the brakes until it is overhead again.**
    - Excerpt: "A full stall occurs when the wing completely loses airflow and deforms" / "Fully release brakes once the canopy is inflated and in front." / "If the canopy has dropped behind the pilot, brakes must be held until it moves overhead again."
    - Source: S12, Bolero 8 User Manual, Gin Gliders
    - Licence: (c) facts only

11. **Release the brakes slowly and evenly at first, and only release them fully once the wing has pre-inflated.**
    - Excerpt: "the brakes should, at first, be released slowly and symmetrically" / "only fully released when pre-inflation is complete"
    - Source: S10, ALPHA 8 manual, Advance
    - Licence: (c) facts only

### Spin (one side stalled)

12. **In a spin, one side stalls while the other keeps flying. To recover, release both brakes and let the glider surge, but never release while the glider is far behind you.**
    - Excerpt: "A spin occurs when one side of the glider stalls while the other continues flying," / "For recovery, fully release the brakes on both sides." / "Never release a spin when the glider is far behind you."
    - Source: S12, Bolero 8 User Manual, Gin Gliders
    - Licence: (c) facts only

13. **Advance's general rule for any loss of control, and especially a one-sided stall: hands up.**
    - Excerpt: "Basically, in all out-of-control flight situations, but especially the onset of a one-sided stall, you should immediately release both brakes fully – hands up!"
    - Source: S10, ALPHA 8 manual, Advance
    - Licence: (c) facts only

14. **The spinning glider rotates around its vertical axis, with one side flying forwards and the other backwards. The slower you fly, the worse the spin tends to be.**
    - Excerpt: "The glider spins on its yaw axis: one wing flying forwards, one backwards." / "The slower you are flying, the worse the spin tends to be."
    - Source: S40, Flybubble
    - Licence: (c) facts only

### Deep (parachutal) stall

15. **A parachutal stall is a steady, almost vertical descent with little or no forward speed. The wing still looks inflated.**
    - Excerpt: "A deep stall is characterized by a stable, near -vertical descent with little or no forward speed."
    - Source: S12, Bolero 8 User Manual, Gin Gliders
    - Excerpt (Ozone): "The glider often looks as though it has recovered properly but carries on descending vertically"
    - Source: S6, Delta 4 manual, Ozone
    - Licence: (c) facts only

16. **The wing is stalled at a high angle of attack but stays inflated because of the vertical descent.**
    - Excerpt: "The wing has stalled due to a high angle of attack but the wing remains inflated due to vertical descent."
    - Source: S40, Flybubble
    - Licence: (c) facts only

17. **Signs of a parachutal stall: less airspeed and wind noise, mushy brakes, and a vertical descent of about 4 to 5 m/s.**
    - Excerpt: "Reduced airspeed and flight noise" / ""Mushy" brake feel ing" / "Vertical descent at approx. 4 -5 m/s"
    - Source: S12, Bolero 8 User Manual, Gin Gliders. The broken spacing is from the PDF text extraction.
    - Licence: (c) facts only

### Causes of parachutal stall

18. **Gin's list of causes: wet or aged fabric, out-of-trim lines, brake lines that are too short or held under tension, and low wing loading.**
    - Excerpt: "Possible causes include:" / "Wet or aged fabric" / "Out-of-trim lines" / "Brake lines too short or held under tension" / "Low wing loading"
    - Source: S12, Bolero 8 User Manual, Gin Gliders
    - Licence: (c) facts only

19. **Ozone's list: a very slow release from a B-line stall, flying when wet, or after a frontal collapse.**
    - Excerpt: "This can be caused by several situations including; a very slow release from a B-line stall; flying the glider when wet; or after a front/symmetric deflation."
    - Source: S6, Delta 4 manual, Ozone
    - Licence: (c) facts only

20. **Wet wing: water beads on the leading edge and separates the airflow.**
    - Excerpt: "water tends to bead on the leading edge causing flow separation"
    - Source: S9, Flying In Rain / Precipitation, Ozone, https://flyozone.com/paramotor/infozone/flying-in-the-rain
    - Licence: (c) facts only

21. **Some wings are more prone to parachutal stall when wet, in every class. Older cloth may be at higher risk because it absorbs water more readily.**
    - Excerpt: "It is known that some canopies have an increased tendency to enter parachutal stalls when wet."
    - Second excerpt: "Occurrences of parachutal stall when wet have been reported throughout all classes."
    - Third excerpt: "It would appear that older canopies, where the cloth can absorb water more readily may be at higher risk"
    - Source: S18, Safety Journal ("Parachutal stall accident", Karl Slezak), DHV
    - Licence: (c) facts only

22. **Porous or aged cloth: an older wing's coating is more permeable, so it absorbs more water.**
    - Excerpt: "older wings will absorb more water than newer ones due to the coating on older wings being more permeable"
    - Source: S14, Lhotse Manual E V1.2, UP International, https://www.up-paragliders.com/images/downloads/LHOTSE/Lhotse_Manual_E_V1.2.pdf
    - Licence: (c) facts only

23. **Line trim: over time the front lines stretch and the rear lines shrink, and wet use speeds this up. Brakes that are too short make recovery hard even on a new glider.**
    - Excerpt: "The front lines stretch, and the rear lines shrink"
    - Second excerpt: "Even a brand new glider will be difficult to recover from a parachutal/steady state stall if the brakes are too short."
    - Source: S47, Vector Nov/Dec 2010 "Life Lines", Civil Aviation Authority of New Zealand, https://aviation.govt.nz/assets/publications/vector/Vector_2010-6_Nov-Dec.pdf
    - Licence: NZ Crown copyright, facts only

24. **Rear risers: over-braking or pulling the rear risers can cause a deep stall.**
    - Excerpt: "It will recover from a deep stall brought about by over-braking, by pulling on the rear risers,"
    - Source: S14, Lhotse manual, UP
    - Supporting source: S15, UP Edge manual (acro wing): "by pulling on the rear risers, or by releasing the B-risers too slowly after a B-stall." https://www.up-paragliders.com/images/downloads/EDGE/EdgeHandb_E.pdf
    - Licence: (c) facts only

25. **Big ears increase the angle of attack, so avoid them when wet or near the stall.**
    - Excerpt (DHV): "manoeuvres which cause the canopy to fly with a greater angle-of-attack (e.g. Big ears) should be avoided" (S18)
    - Excerpt (Ozone): "with a wet wing this will further increase the chances of a parachutal stall occurring." (S7, LM7, about big ears)
    - Excerpt (Ozone, Big Ears section): "Avoid deep symmetric applications of the brake as this could accidently induce parachutal or full stalls." (S6)
    - Licence: (c) facts only

26. **Big ears and speed bar, Ozone: pull big ears first and then add speed bar to sink faster. Never pull big ears when already accelerated, because the lower angle of attack plus the deflating tips can cause a major collapse.**
    - Excerpt: "Always make the Big Ears first and then apply the speed bar." / "The lower angle of attack and the act of deflating the tips can lead to a major deflation."
    - Source: S6, Delta 4 manual, Ozone
    - Licence: (c) facts only

### What to do

27. **Recovery, Ozone and Gin: raise both hands fully, then push the A-risers forward or apply speed bar.**
    - Excerpt (Ozone): "your first reaction should be to fully raise both hands." / "reach up and push the A-risers forwards or apply the speed bar to encourage the wing to regain normal flight." (S6)
    - Excerpt (Gin): "For recovery, e nsure brakes are fully released ." / "If necessary, apply speed bar or gently push the A -risers forward." (S12)
    - Licence: (c) facts only

28. **Never brake in a parachutal stall. Even a few centimetres can turn it into a full stall, and if you must land in one, do not use the brakes at all.**
    - Excerpt (Gin): "Never apply brake during deep stall." (S12)
    - Excerpt (DHV): "applying a few centimetres of brake may result in the canopy going into an uncontrollable full stall." (S18)
    - Excerpt (DHV): "If a landing while in a parachutal stall is unavoidable, it is most important that the brakes are not used at all" (S18)
    - Excerpt (UP): "Avoid applying brake to one side if you think that you are in a deep stall as this could lead to a spin." (S14)
    - Licence: (c) facts only

29. **Wet wing, Advance: if a wet glider goes parachutal, recover only with the speed bar.**
    - Excerpt: "If the wet glider were to go into parachutal stall you should recover only by accelerating using the speed bar."
    - Source: S10, ALPHA 8 manual, Advance
    - Supporting source: S9, Ozone rain page: "If your wing enters parachutal stall when wet, immediately release the trimmers and accelerate the wing to regain airspeed"
    - Licence: (c) facts only

30. **After a deep stall, have the glider and its line trim checked before flying it again.**
    - Excerpt: "After a deep stall, the glider and line trim must be inspected by an authorized service center before further flight."
    - Source: S12, Bolero 8 User Manual, Gin Gliders
    - Licence: (c) facts only

### Disagreements and cautions (Q2)

- **"Deep stall" vs "parachutal stall":**
  - Ozone, DHV and Advance say "parachutal stall".
  - Gin calls the same thing "deep stall".
  - Niviuk (S13) uses both terms differently. Its "parachutal stall" is the inflated, no-pressure state. Its "deep stall" is a stall from flying very slowly while over-steering, and the advice there is to ease the brakes up to half travel.
  - Do not merge Niviuk's "deep stall" advice into the parachutal-stall advice.
- **Using the A-risers:**
  - Ozone and Gin say push them forward.
  - Niviuk says "manually push on the A-lines" without braking (S13).
  - The CAA NZ Vector article says "push forward, or pull down gently" (S47).
  - Apco's F7 manual (S51) says to pull the A-risers down: "The best way to exit a parachutal stall is to pull all the A risers down to get the wing". It also gives the speed bar as an alternative.
  - Advance says that for a wet wing you should use only the speed bar.
  - In the DHV accident report, both standard methods failed: the pilot could not recover "using the standard methods (pulling the A-risers or accelerating with the speed bar)" (S18).
  - On the page, avoid implying that any one method always works.
- **Manufacturer claims for single models:**
  - Advance says of the ALPHA 8: "Stable parachutal stall cannot be established, whether attempted by brake or B-stall." (S10)
  - Niviuk says parachutal stall is "virtually impossible" on the HOOK 6 (S13).
  - Both are claims about one model, not facts about paragliders in general.
- **Aspect ratio definition:** the FAA glossary defines it as span divided by average chord (S1). NASA defines it as span squared divided by area (S5). These are the same thing because average chord = area / span. See Q5.

### Could not verify (Q2)

- **The FAA PHAK chapter 5 PDF itself was not fetched.** The URL never appeared in a search result. The required statement comes from the PHAK Glossary (S1), and AC 61-67C (S2) and the Glider Flying Handbook (S3) back it up. If the page must cite "PHAK chapter 5" by name, it needs a manual check. The current edition is FAA-H-8083-25C (seen on the faa.gov PHAK landing page).
- **"Porous cloth" as a stated cause.** The closest sources say "aged fabric" (S12), "older canopies, where the cloth can absorb water more readily" (S18) and the coating "being more permeable" (S14). No fetched manual uses the word "porous" as a parachutal-stall cause.

## Q3. What is active flying in paragliding?

### Definition

1. **Active flying means keeping the wing directly overhead with steady internal pressure.**
   - Excerpt: "The goal is to maintain the wing directly overhead with a constant level of internal pressure."
   - Source: S6, Delta 4 manual EN v1.0, Ozone, https://cdn1.flyozone.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/1/2018/07/Delta-4-manual-EN-v1.0.pdf
   - Licence: (c) facts only

2. **The method is to hold a constant pressure in the brakes.**
   - Excerpt: "The aim of active flying is to maintain a constant pressure through the brakes"
   - Source: S6 (Delta 4) and S7 (LM7), Ozone
   - Licence: (c) facts only

3. **Active piloting is a way of feeling what the wing is doing.**
   - Excerpt: "Active piloting is a method of feeling what is happening to the wing."
   - Source: S35, Active Flying (Bruce Goldsmith), Cross Country Magazine, https://xcmag.com/other/active-flying/
   - Licence: (c) facts only

### Feeling the wing

4. **Fly with a little brake on, so you can feel feedback from the wing. Ozone suggests about 20 cm; Gin suggests about the weight of your arms.**
   - Excerpt (Ozone): "Flying with a small amount of brake applied (approx. 20cm) will allow you to feel the feedback from the wing." (S6)
   - Excerpt (Gin): "Maintain light, constant brake tension, approximately equal to the weight of your arms." (S12, Bolero 8 User Manual, https://static.gingliders.com/paragliders/bolero-8/documents/bolero-8-user-manual-en.pdf)
   - Licence: (c) facts only

5. **Goldsmith's aim is the same weight and pressure on the brake line at all times.**
   - Excerpt: "trying to maintain the same weight and pressure on the brake line at all times."
   - Source: S35, Cross Country Magazine
   - Licence: (c) facts only

### Pressure control

6. **If pressure drops, brake quickly until it is back to normal, on one side or both.**
   - Excerpt: "If you feel a reduction or loss of pressure quickly apply the brakes until you feel normal pressure again." / "The brake inputs can be symmetric or asymmetric; you may have to apply both brakes or just one."
   - Source: S6, Delta 4 manual, Ozone
   - Licence: (c) facts only

7. **Braking on the side that is losing pressure raises its angle of attack and can stop the collapse before it happens.**
   - Excerpt: "To prevent the collapse from happening, pull the brake handle on the affected side of the wing." / "It will increase the incidence of the wing (angle of attack)."
   - Source: S13, HOOK 6 user manual, Niviuk, https://niviuk.com/biblioteca/items/449_A/user_manual_hook_6_glider_niviuk_paragliders.pdf
   - Licence: (c) facts only

### Pitch control

8. **If the wing pitches in front of you, brake to slow it. If it drops behind, release the brakes so it speeds up, and be ready for the next surge forward.**
   - Excerpt: "If the glider pitches in front of you, use the brakes to slow it down." / "Equally, if the glider drops behind you, release the brakes to allow it to speed up, but be ready to anticipate the following pitch forward."
   - Source: S7, LM7 manual EN v1.0, Ozone, https://cdn1.flyozone.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/1/2019/12/LM7-manual-EN-v1.0.pdf (the same wording is in S6)
   - Licence: (c) facts only

9. **Make the correction and then give the wing its speed back. Holding the brakes too long can stall it.**
   - Excerpt: "Do not correct the glider (braking) for too long in case this provokes a stall." / "If you have to take corrective action, make the input then re-establish the correct flying speed."
   - Source: S13, HOOK 6 manual, Niviuk
   - Licence: (c) facts only

10. **Do not hold deep brake for long in rough air.**
    - Excerpt: "Avoid flying with continuous amounts of deep brake in rough air as you could inadvertently stall the wing"
    - Source: S6, Delta 4 manual, Ozone (the same line is in S8, Atom 3)
    - Licence: (c) facts only

### Why it prevents most collapses

11. **These small corrections keep the wing flying smoothly overhead and greatly reduce the chance of a collapse.**
    - Excerpt: "These subtle adjustments will keep the glider flying smoothly and directly above you and dramatically reduce the likelihood of a collapse."
    - Source: S7, LM7 manual, Ozone
    - Licence: (c) facts only

12. **Ozone calls active flying essential for reducing collapses in turbulence.**
    - Excerpt: "To minimize the likelihood of suffering collapses in turbulent conditions, it is essential to use active flying."
    - Source: S6, Delta 4 manual, Ozone
    - Licence: (c) facts only

13. **Niviuk recommends active flying in turbulence to keep control and prevent collapses.**
    - Excerpt: "We recommend active flying in turbulent conditions, always taking measures to maintain control of the wing, preventing it from collapsing and restoring the speed required by the wing after each correction."
    - Source: S13, HOOK 6 manual, Niviuk
    - Licence: (c) facts only

14. **Schools say the same.**
    - Excerpt (SkyNomad): "Active flying greatly reduces collapses in paragliding" (S45, https://www.skynomad.com/dealing-with-big-collapses)
    - Excerpt (Flybubble): "Controlling your pitch and roll all the time will reduce these collapses and make your flying more fun." (S40, https://flybubble.com/blog/paraglider-control-stall-spin-collapse)
    - Excerpt (XC Mag 2006): "Active piloting is the main form of defence against collapses." (S36)
    - Licence: (c) facts only

15. **It reduces collapses but does not remove them.**
    - Excerpt: "No pilot and no glider are immune to collapses however correct active flying will reduce the chances significantly."
    - Source: S6, Delta 4 manual, Ozone
    - Licence: (c) facts only

16. **Federation framing: the DHV's descriptions of the higher classes assume an active pilot.**
    - LTF-C excerpt: "Designed for pilots familiar with recovery techniques, who fly "actively" and regularly,"
    - LTF-D excerpt: "Designed for pilots well practised in recovery techniques, who fly very actively,"
    - Source: S16, Classification, DHV, https://www.dhv.de/en/type-inspection/classification/
    - Licence: (c) facts only

### Riser steering variant (B/C risers on performance wings)

17. **Some performance wings let you fly actively on the risers instead of the brakes while gliding. Ozone's LM7 system acts on both the B and C risers.**
    - Excerpt: "Whilst gliding it is possible to pilot the wing with the risers, this gives an improved feel and control over the wing enabling you to fly actively without using the brakes."
    - Source: S7, LM7 manual, Ozone
    - Licence: (c) facts only

18. **Why risers instead of brakes when accelerated: braking at speed adds drag and weakens the profile. The risers raise the angle of attack more evenly across the chord.**
    - Excerpt: "Using the risers increases the angle of attack more evenly across the chord and does not weaken the profile as much as using the brakes."
    - Source: S7, LM7 manual, Ozone
    - Licence: (c) facts only

19. **Technique: if the leading edge loses pressure, ease off the speed bar and press on the C risers. Use only small inputs, or you can stall part or all of the wing. Always release the speed bar before any C input.**
    - Excerpt: "Be careful to use only small inputs with the C risers, you risk stalling part or all of the wing if you are over enthusiastic." / "always release the speed bar first before making any C inputs."
    - Source: S7, LM7 manual, Ozone
    - Licence: (c) facts only

20. **Limits: riser control is for gliding in normal air. In strong turbulence, go back to trim speed and fly actively with the brakes.**
    - Excerpt: "This control method is suitable for gliding in good 'normal' air, it does not replace proper active flying with the brakes in strong turbulent conditions."
    - Source: S7, LM7 manual, Ozone
    - Licence: (c) facts only

21. **Designers expect pilots of 2-liners to be comfortable with active C-steering.**
    - Excerpt: "If you are an old school pilot who is not used to being active with C-steering then TRANGO X is a great choice for you."
    - Source: S37, Franta and Jarda talk, UP International, https://www.up-paragliders.com/news/franta-jarda-talk-2-and-2-5-liner-designs
    - Licence: (c) facts only

### Disagreements and cautions (Q3)

- **Weight shift as part of active flying.** The manuals describe active flying almost entirely as brake (or riser) input. Weight shift comes in mainly during collapse recovery (Q1 facts 21 to 23). Flybubble's "pitch and roll" wording (S40) is the closest support for including roll and weight shift in the definition. Keep weight shift as a supporting element, not the core.
- **Ozone also advises not flying in turbulent conditions at all.** "We strongly advise you to keep hold of your brakes at all times and to not fly in turbulent conditions." (S6). Active flying is a skill for coping with turbulence, not permission to seek it out.

### Could not verify (Q3)

- **No BHPA or USHPA active-flying article was fetched** because of the search limit. The school and federation sources are Flybubble (S40, S41), SkyNomad (S45) and DHV class texts (S16). XC Mag (S35) is a magazine extract of a book by a former world champion.

2. Ozone, LM7 manual ("Active Riser Control") (S7)
3. Niviuk, HOOK 6 user manual (S13)
4. Flybubble, "Paraglider Control: Stall, Spin, Collapse!" (S40)
5. SkyNomad Paragliding School, "Dealing with big collapses" (S45)

### The class descriptions

The final EN 926-2:2013+A1:2021 text is paywalled and was not read. The EN wording below comes from the 2012 CEN working draft (S25), and a club revision sheet (S31) matches it. DHV's LTF wording (S16) differs for class A; see the disagreements below.

7. **EN A.** Flight characteristics: maximum passive safety and very forgiving handling. Pilots: everyone, including students at any stage of training.
   - Excerpt: "Paragliders with maximum passive safety and extremely forgiving flying characteristics." / "Designed for all pilots including pilots under all levels of training."
   - Source: S25, prEN 926-2:2012 draft (Table 1), CEN/TC 136/WG 6, hosted by Cross Country Magazine, https://xcmag.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/08/DraftEN926-2.pdf
   - Licence: (c) facts only

8. **EN B.** Flight characteristics: good passive safety and forgiving handling. Pilots: everyone; students only if the manufacturer recommends it.
   - Excerpt: "Paragliders with good passive safety and forgiving flying characteristics."
   - Pilot excerpt (DHV, same idea): "Designed for all pilots and may be suitable for pilots under training if recommended by the manufacturer."
   - Source: S25 (draft) and S16 (DHV)
   - Licence: (c) facts only

9. **EN C.** Flight characteristics: moderate passive safety, with possibly dynamic reactions to turbulence and pilot errors; recovery may need precise pilot input. Pilots: those who know recovery techniques, fly actively and regularly, and understand what reduced passive safety means.
   - Excerpt: "Paragliders with moderate passive safety and with potentially dynamic reactions to turbulence and pilot errors." / "Recovery to normal flight may require precise pilot input."
   - Pilot excerpt (DHV): "and understand the implications of flying a glider with reduced passive safety."
   - Source: S25 and S16
   - Licence: (c) facts only

10. **EN D.** Flight characteristics: demanding, with possibly violent reactions to turbulence and pilot errors; recovery needs precise pilot input. Pilots: well practised in recovery, fly very actively, have a lot of experience in turbulent conditions, and accept the implications.
    - Excerpt: "Paragliders with demanding flying characteristics and potentially violent reactions to turbulence and pilot errors." / "Recovery to normal flight requires precise pilot input."
    - Pilot excerpt (DHV): "have significant experience of flying in turbulent conditions, and who accept the implications of flying such a wing."
    - Source: S25 and S16
    - Licence: (c) facts only

### How the test works

11. **The overall class is the highest (most demanding) result from any single test manoeuvre.**
    - Excerpt (EN draft): "The class of a paraglider according to this document is determined by the highest classification obtained." (S25)
    - Excerpt (DHV): "The overall classification results from the highest (= most demanding) rating" (S16)
    - Licence: (c) facts only

12. **The tests rate flight characteristics only, not performance.**
    - Excerpt: "These tests only make statements about the flight characteristics, not the flight performance."
    - Source: S16, DHV
    - Licence: (c) facts only

13. **The tests are flown in calm, non-turbulent air.** The 2012 EN draft set wind under 10 km/h and no turbulence in the test area.
    - Excerpt: "wind less than 10 km/h within the test perimeter" / "no turbulence within the test perimeter disturbing the flight tests"
    - Source: S25, prEN 926-2:2012 draft (5.4)
    - Licence: (c) facts only

14. **Test pilots induce the collapses on purpose by pulling lines.** The 2012 draft's asymmetric test reads: pull the lines on one side as fast as possible.
    - Excerpt: "Pull down the appropriate lines on one side as fast as possible."
    - Source: S25 (5.5.18.14)
    - Supporting source: S18, DHV, on an old test standard: "asymmetric collapse: 75% of the leading edge along a 45 degree collapse line"
    - Licence: (c) facts only

15. **Two test pilots fly the full programme, one at the minimum and one at the maximum declared flying weight.**
    - Excerpt: "one at the minimum weight in flight declared by the manufacturer, the other one at the maximum."
    - Source: S25
    - Licence: (c) facts only

16. **The Swiss federation says even the EN working group regards the flights as comparisons under lab-like conditions, not a safety guarantee.** It adds that test collapses on modern, heavily reinforced wings are drifting further from reality, especially from High B upwards.
    - Excerpt (French): "Même le WG 6 (...) reconnaît que les tests de vol EN doivent être considérés davantage comme des vols comparatifs dans des conditions de laboratoire que comme une garantie de sécurité."
    - Translation: "Even WG 6 (...) recognises that EN flight tests should be seen more as comparative flights under laboratory conditions than as a guarantee of safety."
    - Second excerpt: "Cela vaut en particulier à partir du niveau High B et au-delà." ("This applies particularly from High B level upwards.")
    - Source: S21, EN class descriptions, SHV/FSVL, https://www.shv-fsvl.ch/fileadmin/files/redakteure/Allgemein/Sicherheit/Sicherheit/EN-Klassen_Beschreibung_FR.pdf
    - Licence: (c) facts only

17. **Most modern wings score A on the 50% asymmetric test from trim speed, so that one test says little when choosing a wing.**
    - Excerpt: "most modern wings score an A on the 50% asymmetric-from-trim-speed test"
    - Source: S41, Paragliders: Safety, Flybubble, https://flybubble.com/blog/paragliders-safety
    - Licence: (c) facts only

18. **Each size is tested and certified separately.** Air Turquoise issued Sky's Fides 3 one certificate per size: S PG_0389.2010, M PG_0383.2010, L PG_0390.2010, XL PG_0391.2010.
    - Excerpt: each report states "Classification A".
    - Source: S23, Air Turquoise certificates for Sky Fides 3, https://sky-cz.com/media/cache/file/4f/Fides3_EN-certification.pdf. The document is OCR text with some garbled dates.
    - Licence: (c) facts only

19. **A real test report lists about 23 numbered test items, each given its own class, and the overall class matches the worst of them.** In 2023 the Apco Nestra L scored a mix of A and B results (B on items 1, 3, 10, 14, 18, 20, 21) and was classed B overall. It was tested to EN 926-1:2015, EN 926-2:2013+A1:2021 and NfL 2-565-20.
    - Source: S24, Air Turquoise classification sticker PG_2189.2023, https://www.apcoaviation.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/PG_2189-2023_ST_NESTRA_L.pdf
    - Licence: (c) facts only

20. **Air Turquoise: a Swiss test house in Villeneuve, founded in 1995, approved by the German aviation authority (LBA) in 2009, and it reports testing more than 2,500 gliders to EN in all four classes.**
    - Excerpt: "We have tested over 2500 gliders according to the EN standard in all categories (A, B, C and D)."
    - Source: S22, Air Turquoise
    - Licence: (c) facts only

### LTF (German equivalent)

21. **In Germany, wings are classified to the national airworthiness requirements, the LTF, using classes LTF A to D.** DHV is an LBA-recognised testing centre. Test reports cite the German rule (NfL 2-565-20) next to EN 926-2 (see S24).
    - Excerpt (DHV): "The latest airworthiness requirements (LTF) apply to the classification of flight characteristics" (S16)
    - Excerpt (LTF-2009): "the airworthiness requirements LTF-2009 publicised by the German Aviation Authorities (LBA)" (S20, https://www.dhv.de/media/seiten/03_musterpruefstelle/Downloads/Lufttuechtigkeitsforderungen/LTF2009_Eng_final_1.pdf)
    - Licence: (c) facts only

22. **History: before the EN/LTF letters, DHV used a 1, 1-2, 2, 2-3, 3 scale.** Class 1 was "simple and very forgiving", up to class 3 "for expert pilots". It used the same highest-result rule.
    - Excerpt: "Paragliders with simple and very forgiving flying characteristics." / "The overall classification of a glider is found by taking the highest"
    - Source: S17, DHV classification of paragliders, https://dhv.de/en/testing/dhv-classification-of-paragliders
    - Licence: (c) facts only

### CCC (CIVL Competition Class)

23. **CCC is the FAI/CIVL class for wings allowed in top (Category 1) cross-country competitions. Its purpose is safe, fair and satisfying competition.**
    - Excerpt: "The purpose of the CIVL Competition Class rules is to permit safe, fair and satisfying competitions."
    - Source: S29, Sporting Code S7G, CCC paragliders requirements 2024, FAI/CIVL, https://www.fai.org/sites/default/files/civl/documents/sporting_code_s7_g_-_ccc_paragliders_requirements_2024.pdf
    - Licence: (c) facts only

24. **CCC builds on EN.** A wing must meet a 23G load requirement. An existing EN 926-2 certificate of the same test wing counts for the flight tests. The flight tests are pass or fail, not graded A to D.
    - Excerpt: "CCC paragliders must be constructed in compliance with the 23G theoretical load test requirements." / "Any existing EN 926-2 certification of the same test specimen implicitly satisfies the flight test requirement" / "Otherwise the test specimen has passed the in-flight tests."
    - Source: S29
    - Licence: (c) facts only

25. **CCC caps top speed: the speed bar may shorten the front risers by at most 140 mm. Only the first size must be certified by a lab; other sizes can be certified by scaling.**
    - Excerpt: "The maximum shortening of the frontmost risers relative to the rearmost riser through the accelerator is 140 mm"
    - Excerpt: "then other sizes certified by linear scaling by a testing laboratory or by a manufacturer."
    - Source: S29
    - Licence: (c) facts only

26. **CCC test conditions: wind under 20 km/h, no turbulence, and testing over water is highly recommended.**
    - Excerpt: "Wind less than 20 km/h within the test perimeter" / "It is highly recommended that all test manoeuvres be carried out over water"
    - Source: S29
    - Licence: (c) facts only

27. **The 2016 CCC update capped aspect ratio at 7.90, the highest flat aspect ratio of any EN-certified glider by 31 Dec 2013. It also limited top speed and shortened the allowed recovery time from 5 to 3 seconds.**
    - Excerpt: "Additionally restrict aspect ratio to the maximum flat aspect ratio exhibited by EN certified gliders by December 31, 2013 (7.90)."
    - Source: S30, What's Up With The New CCC Paragliders?, FAI, https://fai.org/node/21590
    - Licence: (c) facts only

### The 2-liner EN C phenomenon

28. **Since about 2021 or 2022, EN C wings may be certified using folding lines, the extra lines test pilots use to induce collapses. Before that, only EN D allowed them.**
    - Excerpt (SHV): "depuis 2021, les ailes de classe C peuvent être homologuées avec des lignes de pliage (aides à la fermeture)." ("since 2021, class C wings can be certified with folding lines (closing aids).") (S21)

    - Excerpt (2012 draft, the earlier rule): "Folding lines shall not be used in categories A, B and C gliders." (S25)
    - Licence: (c) facts only

29. **As a result, EN C is increasingly dominated by 2-liners.**
    - Excerpt: "Par conséquent, cette classe est progressivement dominée par des ailes à deux rangées de suspentes." ("As a result, this class is progressively dominated by wings with two rows of lines.")
    - Source: S21, SHV/FSVL
    - Licence: (c) facts only

30. **A designer confirms that folding lines are what let 2-liners into EN C.**
    - Excerpt: "Currently, the folding lines are allowed also for EN-C class, so the 2-liners can be squeezed also here."
    - Source: S37, Franta and Jarda talk, UP International (Frantisek Pavlousek, chief designer)
    - Licence: (c) facts only

31. **Some wings that would once have been low EN D now get a C rating.**
    - Excerpt: some former low EN-D wings "started to get a C rating in certification."
    - Source: S32, Cross Country Magazine
    - Licence: (c) facts only

32. **What a folding line is: in effect another A-riser with some slack in it. It lets the pilot make a collapse of the size the test requires. Critics question how realistic that collapse is.**
    - Excerpt: "effectively another A-riser, but with some slack in it"
    - Source: S34, Folding lines under fire..., Cross Country Magazine (2012), https://xcmag.com/news/folding-lines-under-fire-as-changes-to-e-n-tests-are-discussed/

### "Low B / high B"

33. **EN B covers a wide range. The Swiss federation splits it into "EN-B «low»", for all pilots including students, and "EN-B «high»", for experienced regular pilots. A high B stays stable in calm air but can surprise the pilot after a collapse.**
    - Excerpt: "Pour tous les pilotes, même en cours de formation" (low B: "For all pilots, even those in training") / "en atmosphère calme, la stabilité reste élevée" (high B: "in calm air, stability remains high")
    - Source: S21, SHV/FSVL

34. **DHV: skill demands vary a lot within EN B. High-B wings handle much more demandingly and are not recommended for pilots who fly irregularly.**

    - Second excerpt: "können für unregelmäßig fliegende Piloten nicht empfohlen werden" ("cannot be recommended for pilots who fly irregularly")
    - Source: S19, DHV class-selection recommendations (German), https://www.dhv.de/media/seiten/02_fliegen/Sicherheit/EmpfehlungenAuswahlGleitschirmklasse.pdf
    - Licence: (c) facts only

    - D: at least about 100 hours a year.
    - Excerpt: "mindestens ca.100 Flugstunden pro Jahr" (D: "at least approx. 100 flying hours per year")

### Disagreements and cautions (Q4)

- **EN A wording:**
  - The 2012 EN draft (S25) and the DHPC sheet (S31) give EN A as "maximum passive safety and extremely forgiving flying characteristics".
  - DHV's current LTF-A text (S16) reads "Paragliders with good passive safety and forgiving flying characteristics. Gliders with some resistance to departures from normal flight." That is almost word for word its LTF-B text.
  - Possible reasons: a DHV transcription quirk, a real LTF difference, or the A1:2021 "modification of paraglider's classification" (S26). It could not be settled without the paywalled final standard.
  - On the page, paraphrase as "the most forgiving class" and avoid quoting either version word for word.
- **EN B pilot skills:** the DHPC sheet (S31) says B is for all pilots "including pilots under all levels of training", the same as A. The 2012 draft and DHV say students only "if recommended by the manufacturer". Use the draft/DHV version.
- **When folding lines were allowed in EN C:** SHV says "since 2021", XC Mag says a 2022 rule change. A1 was published Dec 2021 and became valid from 31 Dec 2021, so it was probably applied from 2022. Say "since around 2022" or "since the 2021 amendment".
- **Wind limits:** 10 km/h in the 2012 EN draft, 20 km/h in the CCC 2024 rules. These are different documents. The wind limit in the final EN text was not verified.
- **CCC aspect ratio cap:** the 2016 FAI announcement (S30) gives 7.90. A full-text search of the 2024 CCC requirements (S29) found no aspect ratio limit. Whether the cap is still in force is unverified, so do not state it as current.
- **Size certification:** under EN, each size has its own test and certificate (S23). Under CCC, only the first size needs a lab, and other sizes can be scaled (S29).

## Q5. What is aspect ratio on a paraglider?

### Definition

1. **Aspect ratio is the span squared divided by the wing area. For a rectangular wing that equals span divided by chord.**
   - Excerpt: "defined to be the square of the span (s) divided by the wing area (A)" / "For a rectangular wing, this reduces to the ratio of the span to the chord length"
   - Source: S5, Wing Geometry, NASA Glenn Research Center, https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/wing-geometry
   - Licence: PD-USGov

2. **The FAA gives the equivalent form: span divided by average chord.**
   - Excerpt: "Span of a wing divided by its average chord."
   - Source: S1, PHAK Glossary, FAA, https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/21_phak_glossary.pdf
   - Supporting source: S3, Glider Flying Handbook ch. 3: "Dividing the wingspan (from wingtip to wingtip) by the average wing chord determines the aspect ratio for a glider."
   - Licence: PD-USGov

3. **Flat vs projected.** "Flat" figures are measured with the wing laid out flat, not inflated; the CIVL rules define flat span that way. Manufacturers also publish a "projected" figure, which is lower because the inflated wing is arched.
   - Excerpt: "All positions are percentages of the flat (i.e. non-inflated) span"
   - Source: S29, CCC requirements 2024, FAI/CIVL
   - Example: the Ozone Delta 4 table lists flat aspect ratio 6 and projected 4.4 for every size (S6).
   - Licence: (c) facts only

### Aerodynamics

4. **For the same area, a high aspect ratio wing has less induced drag.**
   - Excerpt: "Wings with high aspect ratio have lower induced drag than wings with low aspect ratio for the same wing area."
   - Source: S4, Induced Drag Coefficient, NASA Glenn Research Center, https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/induced-drag-coefficient
   - Licence: PD-USGov

5. **Induced drag comes from the tip vortices that form at the wingtips.**
   - Excerpt: "a pair of counter-rotating vortices are formed at the wing tips."
   - Source: S4, NASA Glenn
   - Licence: PD-USGov

6. **Higher aspect ratio gives lower drag, a better lift-to-drag ratio and a better glide. That is why gliders have long spans.**
   - Excerpt: "A higher aspect ratio gives a lower drag, a higher lift to drag ratio, and a better glide angle."
   - Second excerpt: "Gliders have a high aspect ratio because the drag of the aircraft depends on this parameter."
   - Source: S5, NASA Glenn
   - Licence: PD-USGov

7. **Glider wings produce a lot of lift at low angles of attack with little induced drag.**
   - Excerpt: "Glider wings have a high aspect ratio, as shown in Figure 3-10, which generates significant lift at low angles of attack with minimal induced drag."
   - Source: S3, Glider Flying Handbook ch. 3, FAA
   - Licence: PD-USGov

8. **Induced drag grows as airspeed falls, so a high aspect ratio helps most at low speed (for example when climbing in thermals).** The rule is from the FAA; the thermalling example is my own inference, not in the source.
   - Excerpt: "Drag caused by the same factors that produce lift; its amount varies inversely with airspeed."
   - Source: S1, PHAK Glossary, FAA
   - Licence: PD-USGov

### Trade-offs on a flexible wing

9. **High aspect ratio, high wing-loading wings are usually harder to launch.**
   - Excerpt: "these wings with very high aspect ratio and high wing loading are usually challenging to launch"
   - Source: S39, Paragliders: Which Class?, Flybubble, https://flybubble.com/blog/paragliders-which-class
   - Licence: (c) facts only

10. **Narrow tips on high-end wings bring a real risk of cravats in collapses. In Flybubble's test, the advanced wing's tips caught in the lines several times.**
    - Excerpt: "with narrow tips that bring a real risk of cravats during collapses."
    - Second excerpt: "The narrow tips on the Advanced wing bend and stall easily, and got stuck a few times in the lines with small cravats."
    - Source: S39, Flybubble
    - Licence: (c) facts only

11. **A higher aspect ratio gives a wider turning arc, and the higher classes leave a much smaller window for safe recovery.**
    - Excerpt: "Sports class wings are designed for higher performance, with a wider turning arc due to their increased aspect ratio and speed."
    - Second excerpt: "as the window for safe recovery is much smaller compared to lower-class wings."
    - Source: S39, Flybubble
    - Licence: (c) facts only

12. **Performance wings pair higher aspect ratio with less line.**
    - Excerpt: "Sports wings typically feature streamlined designs, such as reduced line consumption and increased aspect ratios and cell counts."
    - Source: S39, Flybubble
    - Licence: (c) facts only

13. **The designer's view of 2-liners: more resistant to collapse and faster, but livelier in bumps.** This is a counterpoint to the "high-end = more collapses" assumption.
    - Excerpt: "The 2-liner concept in general is more resistant to collapses," / "the 2-liner jumps a few meters up in almost every single bump you go through."
    - Source: S37, Franta and Jarda talk, UP International
    - Licence: (c) facts only

14. **Typical flat aspect ratio by class, from one retailer's comparison:**
    - First 4.8
    - Progression 5.2
    - XC 5.5
    - Sports 6.1
    - Performance 7.0
    - Competition 7.6
    - Source: S39, Flybubble (example table)
    - Licence: (c) facts only

### Disagreements and cautions (Q5)

- **Two forms of the definition.** NASA uses span squared over area; FAA uses span over average chord. These are the same thing because average chord = area / span, so this is not a real disagreement. Use span squared over area on the page and mention the chord version for intuition.
- **"Higher AR = more collapse-prone" is too simple.** Flybubble links narrow tips to cravats and a smaller recovery window (S39). UP's designer says 2-liners resist collapse better (S37). Flybubble also notes that collapse-resistant wings can recover more violently (S41). Frame high aspect ratio as more demanding when things go wrong, not as more fragile.

### Could not verify (Q5)

- **More span-wise flexibility at higher aspect ratio.** No fetched source says this.
- **Line drag as a historical limit on aspect ratio.** Only indirect support: Flybubble says sports wings have reduced line consumption (S39), and UP compares line drag of 2 vs 2.5-liners (S37). No source ties line drag to aspect ratio limits.
- **A sourced definition of "projected" aspect ratio.** No fetched source defines "projected" in words. The explanation in fact 3 (inflated, arched wing) is mine and should be checked against a manufacturer glossary before publication.
- **Spec tables from manufacturer websites for the D and CCC wings.** The Zeno 2 and Enzo 3 figures come from a retailer that reproduces Ozone's tables, plus Wikipedia for the Enzo. flyozone.com product pages never appeared in search results. The LM7 (EN D) figure does come from Ozone's own manual.
- **Some models are older.** Delta 4 (2018), LM7 (2019) and Atom 3 (2018) are past models. Gin Bolero 8 (2026), Niviuk HOOK 6 and Ozone Zeno 2 are recent. Swap in current models later if wanted.
