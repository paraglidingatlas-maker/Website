Your first morning

It is your first morning at Bir. The valley is already warm. Four of you share a taxi up the hill, about 45 minutes of hairpins, and step out on the meadow at Billing at 2,400 m with the Dhauladhar standing over you and a queue of wings laid out along the grass. There is no wind to speak of. There almost never is. You lay out to the side, wait for your gap, and go.

By early afternoon you are at cloudbase, over 3,000 m, where it is freezing, looking down a front range that works for tens of kilometres in either direction and up at the snow of the back ranges. By the end of the day you are picking a field at the edge of town with the light going and the whole valley turning gold.

That is the trip. What follows is everything between now and that morning: the paperwork that cannot be done on the day, the nine things pilots who have flown here for decades want you to know, what to pack, and what Bir is when you are not in the air. Read it once now. Keep it for the week before you leave.

Paperwork first
Nothing here can be done on the day
 
Visa
Most nationalities can use the e-visa, applied for at least 4 days before you arrive. It suits most nationalities, but not all, so check yours early.
 
e-Arrival Card
Everyone files a free online e-Arrival Card in the 72 hours before flying in.
 
Passport
Valid for 6 months, with two blank pages.
 
Licence
Your IPPI 2 card or national licence. This is a tour for autonomous pilots.
 
Insurance, essential
Travel and accident insurance valid for paragliding at the altitudes flown on this trip, covering search and rescue including helicopter, mountain rescue, medical treatment and repatriation. Standard travel insurance often excludes adventure sports, so read the policy wording. Save the policy number and the rescue hotline offline: you will want them when you are down the back with no signal.
The flying
What this trip is actually like

Bir has a reputation as the easy Himalaya, and in the air it mostly earns it. What catches pilots out is everything around the flying: a nil-wind launch with a queue behind you, a house thermal full of wings, and a long approach into a busy landing field. Autumn at Bir now draws hundreds of visiting pilots, so you will share the sky to get it.

It is built for autonomous pilots who want the Himalaya at its most approachable. You can launch cleanly in nil wind, without help from the breeze; you are comfortable thermalling in company and keep looking around while you climb; and you can choose a landing early and fly a full approach at the end of a long flight.

Flying deep into the back ranges on a first visit takes a couple of hundred hours, an SIV course and kit for a night out, so that is not this trip. It is warm in the valley and freezing at cloudbase, on the same day. And the plan follows the conditions, not the other way round: there is no fixed timetable.

A day at Bir
How a day runs
01
 
Briefing
The forecast, the plan and the traffic: where the climbs are likely to be, which way to head, and what to do if the day shuts down early.
02
 
Up the hill
Shared taxis up to Billing, about 45 minutes from Bir, the way everyone does it, usually four pilots to a car.
03
 
Launch
Almost always nil-wind, and there will be a queue. Lay out to the side, and once your wing is up, go.
04
 
Fly
Leave the house thermal as soon as you have the height for the next knob. The front range works for tens of kilometres either way, so there is no need to stay in the crowd.
05
 
Land
Most days end back in Bir. Pick your field early, fly a full approach, and anywhere else, watch for terraces and power lines.

Nine rules
From pilots who have flown here for decades

The weather at Bir is the easy part. What the pilots who know it talk about is traffic, and the accidents they describe are traffic accidents. These nine come from two episodes of the show, with Eddie Colfox, who has run trips from Bir since 2007, and Jigish Gohil, who has lived there for about a decade. Their words are paraphrased, not quoted.

01
The wing you bring
Fly something you can relax on
Bir is not the place to find out what your new high end wing does in the afternoon. The flying is long rather than difficult, and the pilots who enjoy it are the ones not busy managing their glider: a wing calm enough that you would happily take a photo while flying it.
Eddie Colfox, paraphrased · India episode, 14:29
02
Before you launch
Tell someone your plan
Say where you are going and who you are going with, every day, before you take off. Then close the loop in the evening. Most of what goes wrong here is survivable; being missing for six hours before anyone notices is what turns it into a rescue.
Eddie Colfox, paraphrased · India episode, 13:39
03
The traffic
Expect company, and a lot of it
The thermals that make Bir easy are the same thermals everyone else is in, and autumn is when everyone comes. When he first came for an autumn season, about 200 pilots from around the world were flying there. In autumn 2023 it was about 800.
Jigish Gohil, paraphrased · Bir episode, 09:11
04
In the thermal
Turn the way everyone else is turning
Join on the outside, take the established direction, and leave room. The mid-airs he describes came from pilots turning the wrong way, bunching up, or not watching where they were going. If the circle gets too tight for your comfort, leave for the next ridge. There is a thermal on every knob along the front range.
Jigish Gohil, paraphrased · Bir episode, 24:39
05
Following
Do not just follow the wing in front
There are exceptional pilots at Bir, and some of them are doing things you should not copy. A line that works for a local on a two liner is not automatically a line for you. Some of the pilots in the air at Bir are superb, and some simply do not know better.
Eddie Colfox, paraphrased · India episode, 14:29
06
The cloud
Watch the sky, not just the climb
Autumn at Bir can overdevelop. On 17 October 2024 a visiting pilot near the end of a long flight was swept from 3,000 m to above 7,000 m inside a storm cloud in about ten minutes, and survived; nine incidents were reported in the area that day. Watch how fast the clouds are building, stay out from under the big ones, and land early rather than late.
Eddie Colfox, paraphrased · India episode, 24:44
07
The landing field
Fly the approach, every time
The Bir landing is big, grassy and busy, which is exactly why people get hurt on it. However big the field, pilots misjudge the last leg, arrive long, then stall the wing in from three or four metres up and break something. Anywhere else, the hazards are terraces and power lines, which show up late.
Jigish Gohil, paraphrased · Bir episode, 30:42
08
Over the back
Going deep is a commitment
Behind the front range the ground gets big and the options get thin. Altitude, thermal strength, roughness and cold all step up at once, and help can take until the next day. His test for going over: a couple of hundred hours, an SIV course, gear to sleep out, and the fitness to walk for eight or nine hours.
Jigish Gohil, paraphrased · Bir episode, 27:35
09
The tracker question
Check what is legal before you pack
Some satellite communicators are not technically legal in India, and the advice online has not all caught up. Ask me before you buy or pack one. Do not find this out at customs.
Eddie Colfox, paraphrased · India episode, 36:49
Bir on the ground
The Himalaya you will not see from the air
A meadow of white flowers below the snow-covered Dhauladhar
Bir's landing field among terraced fields, seen from the air
The Kangra valley from the air, villages and fields below the hills
Tibet in the foothills
Bir is a Tibetan settlement as much as a flying town. The monasteries, the prayer flags on the ridges and the momos in the cafes are not decoration; they are the place. Walk the colony on a rest day and let it be slow.
Dharamshala, under your flight
The run west along the front ends above Dharamshala, home of the Dalai Lama in exile. You will fly over it; take a day to go there on the ground too.
The valley
Palampur, on the map under the route, is Kangra tea country, and the landing field sits among terraces that have been farmed for generations. The ride back from a landing out goes through all of it, and it is part of the trip.
Last light
Most days end at the Bir landing with the sun going down behind the ridge. Land early enough to watch it.

Practical
Getting there and getting by
Getting there
From New Delhi, Bir is an overnight bus ride north, or a short flight to Dharamshala and about two hours by road.
Money
Indian rupees. Bir has only one or two ATMs, and they can run dry at weekends, so arrive with cash in small notes.
Language
Hindi and English. English is widely spoken around Bir, which has welcomed visiting pilots for decades.
Power
230 volts, 50 hertz, plug types C, D and M. Bring an adapter.
