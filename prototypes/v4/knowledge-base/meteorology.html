Can a cloud suck you in when paragliding?

Not in the way the phrase suggests, says Will Gadd: nobody is sucked into a cloud, they fly in, and once you own that, you can fix it by leaving earlier and checking the radar on your phone. In the Himalaya, Eddie Colfox's advice is to be very careful of cloud, and never to follow someone into one just because they are there.

How can you tell a day will overdevelop?

Watch the clock and the sounding. Eddie Colfox notes what time the clouds form, because early clouds usually mean they will grow big later. On a sounding, Ivelin Kalushkov looks at the dew point: if it runs close to the temperature line over a great height, clouds can grow high enough for storms or rain, while a very dry layer stops them overdeveloping.

How do gust fronts catch pilots out?

Often at launch. Eddie Colfox saw one coming across the water just as he turned to launch on the coast, after an older pilot had told him it was too windy; he was lifted, took a big collapse and stalled onto the slope. Ivelin Kalushkov treats any rain radar colour above green, on a day with thunderstorms forecast, as a sign of rain and possible gust fronts.

When is the wind strongest at a flying site?

Often late in the day. In Ivelin Kalushkov's forecast for Manilla in Australia, the wind was expected to build to gusts of 30 km/h late in the afternoon, so his advice was to be in the air before 3 pm. At Roldanillo in Colombia, the afternoon wind makes landing near town gusty, so Pal Takats lands out near the main road instead.

Why can local pilots read the weather better than a forecast?

Because some effects never reach the models. At Manilla, when the wind blows off Lake Keepit, the lake spills cool air into the valley in front of launch from about one o'clock and breaks up the thermals, which no model shows. At Sopot in Bulgaria, strong daytime anabatic winds can overcome a forecast northerly. Ivelin Kalushkov's advice: use local knowledge, and research a site first.
