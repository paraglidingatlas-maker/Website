Thermal Strength

The variometer readings vary from 1-2 m/s (weak, early morning/late afternoon) to 4-6 m/s (strong, midday peaks), with rare surges to 6-7 m/s. Variations include narrow, turbulent cores and wide, smooth ones as well, types range from classic bubble thermals to dynamic triggers depending on the location of flight

Flying Season

The weather in Kenya is determined by its tropical location and the oscillating effects of the Inter-tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), which annually travels back and forth across equatorial Africa, providing Kenya with a bimodal annual rainfall pattern. Flying season peaks in this equatorial paradise from December to March, Ideal for joining our Kenya tour.

The sites are the six of the tour, the launches are read from the owner's own tracklogs and the towns are placed from GeoNames (CC BY 4.0); the parks, peoples, prehistoric sites and landmarks along the way are placed by hand at the centre of each area, with a few lines each from published sources; the border is Natural Earth's. Landmark

The Kerio flows north along the floor of the Rift between the Elgeyo escarpment and the Tugen Hills, about a thousand metres below Iten. The east face of the escarpment is the wall pilots soar, and the long flights run north and south along it.

Wildlife

A park of red cliffs, gorges and steam vents that can be crossed on foot or by bicycle among zebra, buffalo and giraffe. Olkaria, inside the park, is where Kenya draws geothermal power from the floor of the Rift.

ZebraZebra, buffalo, gazelle and giraffe graze the floor of the gorge among walkers and cyclists, which is possible because the park has no lions. It was gazetted in 1984.

Landmark

A young stratovolcano with an intact crater nearly two kilometres across, climbed from the road in a few hours and walked around the rim in a few more. Its forest fills the crater floor.

Landmark

The eastern wall of the Rift above Mai Mahiu, with the old escarpment road below. At its foot stands the small chapel built by Italian prisoners of war in 1942 while they cut that road.

People

Machakos is the heart of Ukambani, the land of the Kamba, known for wood carving and basketry. The town was the first administrative centre of British East Africa, before Nairobi was founded.

Wildlife

A range of young volcanic cones and lava flows, the latest of them from the 1850s, with some of the longest lava tubes in the world beneath. The rain that falls here sinks through the lava and surfaces at Mzima Springs.

Wildlife

A soda lake ringed by acacia woodland, famous for its flamingos and now a fenced sanctuary for black and white rhino. With Elementaita and Bogoria it forms the Kenya Lake System, a World Heritage Site since 2011.

FlamingosThe flamingos feed on the algae that bloom in the soda water. Their numbers rise and fall with the lake: in the high water since 2013 most of them moved on to Bogoria, and they return as the lake drops.

RhinoBlack rhino were brought back to Nakuru after the park was fenced as a sanctuary in the late 1980s, and white rhino followed. It is one of the oldest rhino sanctuaries in Kenya.

Wildlife

A deep soda lake under the Siracho escarpment, with hot springs and geysers along its shore and, in a good season, vast flocks of lesser flamingo. Part of the Kenya Lake System World Heritage Site.

Lesser flamingosLesser flamingos feed on the blue-green algae of the hot soda water, and when Nakuru is too fresh for them they gather here in their hundreds of thousands. Counts at Bogoria have passed a million.

Wildlife

A freshwater lake on the floor of the Rift with hippo, crocodile and well over four hundred recorded bird species. The Njemps who fish it are among the few cattle-keeping peoples of the Rift who also take to the water.

HippoHippo spend the day in the water and graze the shore at night; crocodiles bask on the banks. Baringo is fresh where its neighbours are soda, which is why both live here and not at Bogoria.

Wildlife

The highest of the Kenyan Rift lakes, and fresh, with hippo in the papyrus and fish eagles in the acacias. Crescent Island, the rim of a drowned crater, is walked on foot among giraffe and zebra.

HippoHippo live in the papyrus along the shore and come out at night to graze. The giraffe and zebra of Crescent Island are walked among on foot, because nothing on the island hunts them.

Wildlife

A shallow soda lake where great white pelicans breed on the islets and flamingos feed, protected within the Soysambu conservancy. The third of the Kenya Lake System World Heritage lakes.

FlamingosFlamingos feed in the shallows, and the islets are one of the few places in East Africa where great white pelicans breed.

Landmark

A volcanic caldera on the edge of Nakuru, about twelve kilometres by eight, formed in a great eruption some eight thousand years ago, its floor a field of old lava. One of the largest calderas in the world.

Landmark

A shield volcano with a crater inside a crater, the inner one ringed by a moat, and a system of lava caves beneath its slopes. Maasai herders graze its outer flanks.

Origins

A dry lake bed in the southern Rift where stone hand axes lie in their thousands, left by early humans over hundreds of thousands of years. Louis and Mary Leakey began excavating here in the 1940s; the site is open, with a small museum.

Origins

A prehistoric site above Gilgil where Louis Leakey found Acheulean hand axes and cleavers in 1928, one of the first such sites known in East Africa. A small museum stands beside the old diatomite mine.

Origins

A Neolithic and Iron Age settlement on a hill outside Nakuru, excavated by Mary Leakey in the late 1930s, with burial mounds and hut floors and a museum in the old farmhouse.

Origins

The hills between the Kerio Valley and Lake Baringo, where the six-million-year-old fossils of Orrorin tugenensis were found in 2000, among the oldest candidates for the human line.

People

A town of runners at 2,400 m on the lip of the Elgeyo escarpment. The arch over its road calls it the Home of Champions: the Kalenjin athletes who train on its red tracks and roads have won a large share of the world's distance medals.

RunnersRunners train on the roads and tracks out of Iten from first light, at 2,400 m. Athletes have trained here in numbers since the 1970s, when Brother Colm O'Connell began coaching at St Patrick's High School.

People

The southern Rift from Suswa to Amboseli is Maasai land: pastoralists whose cattle, red cloth and beadwork are the best known image of Kenya, and who still move their herds across it.

Maasai cattleCattle are wealth and food to the Maasai, who moved south along the Rift between the fifteenth and the eighteenth centuries and still herd across it, from Kajiado to Amboseli and beyond.

People

The slopes between the Aberdares and Mount Kenya are the heartland of the Kikuyu, the largest of Kenya's peoples: tea and coffee country, and the ground of the Mau Mau uprising of the 1950s, with Nyeri at its centre.

People

Along the northern Elgeyo escarpment the Marakwet have watered the valley floor for centuries with furrows cut along the cliffs, a system still in use and still managed by the clans.

Wildlife

A small reserve on the floor of the Kerio Valley that elephants pass through on an old corridor up and down the Rift. Across the river, Lake Kamnarok was once thick with crocodiles; it has shrunk badly in recent years.

ElephantsElephants pass through Rimoi on an old route up and down the Kerio Valley, and it was to keep that route open that the reserve was set aside in 1983.

Wildlife

Elephants under Kilimanjaro: a park of swamps and dust where the longest-running study of wild elephants has followed the same families since 1972.

ElephantsThe elephants come to Amboseli for its swamps, fed by water that falls on Kilimanjaro and surfaces here in a dry plain. The families of this park have been followed by name since 1972, the longest study of wild elephants anywhere.

Wildlife

Springs in Tsavo West fed by rain that fell on the Chyulu Hills and filtered through their lava, pouring out clear enough to watch hippo and crocodile from an underwater hide.

HippoHippo spend the day in the clear pools, and their dung feeds the fish that live on it, a chain the filmmakers Alan and Joan Root followed underwater here.

Landmark

Africa's highest mountain at 5,895 m, across the border in Tanzania, its ice on the horizon from the Chyulu Hills on a clear morning.

Landmark

The second highest mountain in Africa at 5,199 m, an old volcano with glaciers on its twin summits, Batian and Nelion. A World Heritage Site.

Wildlife

The only national park inside a capital city, gazetted in 1946 as Kenya's first, where rhino and lion graze against the skyline.

LionLion hunt the plains within sight of the city's towers, and the park is one of Kenya's oldest sanctuaries for black rhino, which bred well enough here to stock other parks.

Landmark

Four knuckles of hill on the edge of the Rift. Karen Blixen farmed at their foot and wrote Out of Africa about it; a wind farm now runs along the ridge.

Landmark

A 74 m fall on the Ewaso Ng'iro at Nyahururu, reached by the Scottish explorer Joseph Thomson in 1883 and named for his father.

Wildlife

A reserve on the Ewaso Ng'iro in dry country north of Mount Kenya, home to the Samburu special five: Grevy's zebra, reticulated giraffe, Somali ostrich, gerenuk and beisa oryx.

Reticulated giraffeThe reticulated giraffe and Grevy's zebra of Samburu are dry-country animals of the north, found in few other places; most of the world's remaining Grevy's zebra live in northern Kenya.

Wildlife

A conservancy on the Laikipia plateau that holds the last two northern white rhinos on earth, Najin and Fatu, under guard day and night.

Najin and FatuNajin and Fatu, the last two northern white rhinos, came here from a zoo in the Czech Republic in 2009; the last male, Sudan, died in 2018. Ol Pejeta also holds more black rhino than any other sanctuary in East Africa.

Landmark

A soda lake at the hot southern end of the Rift, its surface a crust of trona that has been mined for over a century, with flamingos on the brine.

FlamingosFlamingos feed in the lagoons at the edge of the lake, where hot springs bring water in over the crust of soda.

Wildlife

A range of moorland and forest where in February 1952 Princess Elizabeth went up to Treetops lodge a princess and came down a queen.

BuffaloBuffalo and elephant come to the salt licks and waterholes under the forest lodges at night, which is what Treetops was built to watch in 1932.

Landmark

The line crosses the road at Nanyuki, where a sign marks it, and again on the roads north of Nakuru. The tour drives across it on the way south from Kerio.

Landmark

Between Kibwezi and Mtito Andei the Mombasa road runs through baobab country, the hot red lowlands of Tsavo. The trees stand bare for most of the year, their swollen trunks holding water through the dry months, and bear their fruit in hanging pods.

BaobabsBaobabs stand in the hot lowlands of Tsavo and along the Mombasa road, where the rains are short and the trees hold water in their trunks. The big ones are counted in centuries, and the oldest dated trees in Africa are over two thousand years old.

01

What skill and fitness level is required?

This is an XC adventure tour for autonomous pilots. The minimum is being able to manage your own takeoffs and outlandings, with strong thermalling and ridge soaring skills recommended. An SIV course is highly desirable for handling collapses and turbulence.

Fitness: good condition for hikes to launch at moderate elevation, gear handling, and four to six hour active flying days in heat. You should be able to trek uneven terrain with a 10 to 15 kg pack.

02

What equipment is provided?

We provide certified rental wings only, for a fixed fee on request, and that is first come first served, so tell us in good time. Group safety items including first aid and retrieve support are covered.

You bring your own EN or LTF certified wing, harness, reserve, helmet and vario or GPS. Hiking boots and thermal layers are recommended.

03

Will I get my own guide?

No, there are no dedicated one to one guides on this tour. Pilots are led by one or two expert instructors at a maximum ratio of one to three. Personalised XC guidance, weather briefings and retrieve help are provided to the group, focused on collective safety and maximising flights.

04

Does the price include travel?

The base price covers local transport by 4WD van, guiding, park entry for flying activities, takeoff fees, and Paragliding Association of Kenya membership. Transfers from the airport to the meetup point in Nairobi can be arranged on request.

05

What insurance do I need?

Travel insurance covering paragliding at altitudes up to 4,000m. It must include helicopter rescue for retrieves from remote regions, and medical evacuation and treatment covering local hospitals plus international repatriation.

Standard travel insurance often excludes adventure sports, so read the wording rather than assuming.

06

What is the cancellation policy?

The deposit is not refunded, and if you cancel there is a termination fee that on a trip like this is usually most of the price, because permits, guiding and local arrangements are committed months ahead. It is calculated as the price less what we save and less anything we recover by reselling your place, and we will show you the working. The balance is due 120 days before departure. This is exactly why we push trip cancellation insurance so hard. Full detail in the booking terms.

Before You Book

What This Trip Is Actually Like

We would rather you read this and decide it is not for you than arrive and find that out.

Explore the Cradle is built for independent pilots with strong glider handling and an appetite for the unfamiliar. We put flight safety first, because that is what keeps the fun factor intact.

How a day runs, and who it is for

Every flight is a good flight, but chasing airtime comes with bumpy air, variable thermal strength and conditions more demanding than you expected. That is part of it. Come willing to push your limits and you leave with a renewed understanding of flying in new terrain.

How a day runs

  1. 01

    Briefing

    Your guide proposes the day's plan to maximise flying, and briefs you on the forecast, the site and the conditions. He suggests cross country directions and where a retrieve is realistic.

  2. 02

    Launch

    He launches first to check the air, or last to help with an awkward takeoff.

  3. 03

    Fly

    We fly the route together, or improvise over the radio when the day has other ideas. Under moderate easterlies, waiting for each other in the air is often simply not possible.

  4. 04

    Land

    You decide where, and you land on your own. Kenya's generous conditions produce long flights, and a long flight means a long retrieve.

  5. 05

    Retrieve

    After you land, you are the one who activates and directs the retrieve driver, because the others may still be in the air.

This trip is for you if

  • You have strong glider handling and an appetite for the unfamiliar.
  • You can take off, fly, decide and land on your own. That is not a formality here, it is what makes the trip work at all.
  • You are willing to push your limits in air that is rougher than you expected, and to call that the adventure rather than a failure of planning.
  • You can improvise, flex and tolerate a young destination whose cross country routes are still being worked out.

It is not for you if

  • You expect polished service and comfort. This is the wrong trip.
  • You expect quiet, picturesque air every day. Kerio and Machakos can be rough through sections of a flight, and near the escarpments the turbulence is heavier than you were expecting.
  • You want to wait for each other in the air. Most of Kenya sits under moderate easterly winds, and that is often simply not possible.
  • You want a fixed timetable. The plan follows the conditions, not the other way round.
Flying Etiquette

Flying In Africa, And How To Do It Well DIY

For pilots going out there under their own steam rather than on our tour. None of it is specific to us, and all of it is worth reading before you book anything, with anyone.

Paragliding is still new across much of the continent, and how visiting pilots behave shapes what the next ones are allowed to do. This is distilled from thirteen years of Nikolay Yotov's exploration in Ethiopia and Kenya. He is also the guest on the Kenya episode, if you would rather hear it in his own words.

What you see

A green field, a clean wind line, somewhere safe to put it down.

What they see

A stranger dropping out of the sky onto their land, unannounced.

01
How you are read

Read the place before you fly it

You may be seen as progress, as wealth, or with suspicion: spying, drones, something military. None of that is unreasonable given the history. Read up on the communities, the security picture and the recent past of anywhere you are heading before you get there.

02
The approach that works

Greet everyone, explain everything

Be open, enthusiastic and positive, and greet everyone, including the unsmiling ones. Ask locally about security, wild animals and access, because that knowledge is not in any guidebook. Explain your plan clearly: misunderstandings about where you intend to take off are the single most common problem.

Hiring porters is usually welcome. In some communities men will not take portering work, and offering a guiding or security role instead is the respectful move rather than pressing the point.

03
Prices and tipping

Agree the price first

Agree a price in advance, even when someone waves it off. Numbers get misheard, and 15, 50 and 500 are easy to confuse in a second language on a windy hillside.

Tip 20 to 50 per cent for good work, and nothing extra for a rip off. Fairness runs both ways. Pay for the things you cannot see, like a path an ancestor cut or the fact that nothing with teeth came near you. Hire from the community closest to the site, or you create jealousy you will not be around to resolve.

04
When you are asked for money

Give work, not cash

Giving cash directly tends to make things worse for the pilot behind you. Share food or buy someone a meal instead. Saying plainly that it would not be fair to give to one person and not the rest is usually understood and accepted.

Better still, give work as a porter or guide, or donate to a local organisation. Avoid handing out your phone number or email, because it turns into requests for money later.

05
Landing protocol

Land away from crowds

Avoid schools, markets and crowds. Land somewhere remote but reachable by a vehicle. Pack fast with a quick pack sack, then deal with yourself and send your location; repack properly later.

People will arrive, and quickly. Greet the children with enthusiasm, find the elders or the leader, and ask them to help keep a clear space. Fly with live tracking and a driver you trust.

06
Staying and moving

Follow your host's lead

In remote areas you will camp or stay in somebody's house. Take your shoes off, wash your feet, compliment the food, and follow your host's lead on who it is appropriate to address. Pay discreetly, often to the woman running the household, and expect around half the price of a standard hotel.

Public transport is crowded and good fun, and your glider bag may double the fare, which is fair. Hitching, offer close to the bus fare and do not haggle below it; going lower is taken as an insult, not a bargain. A hired driver who negotiates, handles gear, keeps crowds back and will drive at night to fetch you is worth 50 to 100 dollars at the end of the trip. Avoid driving at night yourself. If you land a long way out, sleep where you landed.

07
Permissions and officials

Ask when it matters

The old rule is that it is better to say sorry than to ask permission, and paragliding is not explicitly illegal where the law does not describe it at all. That logic runs out in tense regions, where being arrested is a real outcome and official permission saves a great deal of time.

Kenya is the easy case. The tourism sector is used to foreigners and no special permits are needed for what we do on this tour.

08
You are the precedent

You are the precedent

There are few enough of us flying here that you are not an anonymous tourist. You are the paraglider pilot that community has met, and possibly the only one they will meet this year. What you do sets the terms for everyone who comes next.
